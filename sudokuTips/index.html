Tips & Strategies

From the right solving mindset to advanced elimination techniques — a structured guide for players at every level.

Solving MindsetBeginnerIntermediateAdvanced

Think Broadly, Not Quickly

The most common mistake in Sudoku is fixating on one cell and forcing an answer. The game rewards a wider lens.

Every cell lives at the intersection of three units — its row, its column, and its 3×3 box. A digit placed anywhere in those units constrains what can appear in your cell.

Stuck on a cell? Step away from it. Scan a different row, column, or box. New placements elsewhere often unblock the cell you left behind.

Section 01 · Beginner

Building the Foundation

Before techniques, before tricks — these three habits separate solvers who improve from those who plateau.

01

Scan Before You Write

Before placing any number, spend 30 seconds scanning rows, columns, and boxes. You're building a mental map of what's already placed — and what's missing.

A quick scan at the start often reveals 3–5 cells you can solve immediately.
02

Eliminate, Don't Guess

For each empty cell, note which digits already appear in its row, column, and 3×3 box. The digits that don't appear are its candidates. Work from what you know.

If a cell has exactly one candidate remaining, it's solved. Write it in.
03

Work the Constraints

Digits that appear many times across the board create tighter constraints elsewhere. If 7 is in six rows, the remaining three rows are highly restricted — start there.

Heavily-placed digits reduce the search space dramatically. Use that.

Section 02 · Intermediate

Named Techniques

Once you can scan and eliminate reliably, these named patterns will let you solve any medium-difficulty puzzle without guessing.

04

Naked Singles

When a cell's only remaining candidate is a single digit, fill it. Then immediately re-scan all adjacent cells — your new placement eliminates candidates from up to 20 neighboring cells.

05

Hidden Singles

A digit is a hidden single when — despite a cell having multiple candidates — that digit can appear in only one cell within a unit (row, column, or box). The number has only one valid home.

06

Naked Pairs

When two cells in the same unit share exactly the same two candidates (and no others), those digits are locked to those cells. Eliminate both candidates from every other cell in that unit.

A note on candidate marking: Intermediate techniques require tracking candidates — the possible digits for each empty cell. Use the in-game notes feature (pencil icon) to pencil in candidates before applying these patterns.

Section 03 · Advanced

Pattern Recognition

These techniques require fully marked candidate grids and systematic pattern search. Master them and no expert puzzle will stop you.

07

Pointing Pairs

If a candidate in a 3×3 box is restricted to a single row or column, that digit must appear in one of those cells. Remove it as a candidate from all other cells in that row or column outside the box.

08

Box/Line Reduction

The inverse: if a candidate appears in only one box along a given row or column, it can't appear anywhere else in that box. Remove it from all other cells within the box.

09

X-Wing

When a candidate appears in exactly two cells in each of two rows, and those cells share the same two columns, the digit must land in one of those four corners. Remove it from both columns everywhere else.
